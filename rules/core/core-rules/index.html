Where each idea comes from: §14.

  1. One roll, many outputs. A single 2d12 roll tells you whether you succeed, how well (Power), and whether something twists (doubles).

  2. Fiction is mechanical. Tags, statuses and Truths give recorded fiction mechanical weight: statuses and Situational Conditions modify the roll, tags supply Power, and Truths decide what is possible (§2, §5, §8). Use only the modifiers a rule names; don’t invent situational maths.

  3. Characters are driven, not just capable. Beliefs, Goals, and Instincts are the primary engine for earning Tychē Points and XP.

2. Core Resolution

2.1 The Roll

Declare the goal first. Before rolling, say what you want to accomplish and how. Agree which Effects can express it and what it takes: the Truth, tier, rating or clock segments needed (§5). Settle this before the roll, and don’t announce a new requirement afterward. If one action can’t accomplish the whole goal, say what a smaller result achieves, or use a clock or a conflict.

Roll 2d12 + Skill + Modifiers vs. Difficulty.

  • Skill: +0 to +4 (from the skill pyramid; untrained = +0).

  • Personal statuses: + the tier of the highest relevant helpful status, − the tier of the highest relevant harmful status (§5.1).

  • Situational Conditions: + the rating of the highest relevant helpful Condition, − the rating of the highest relevant harmful one (§5.2).

  • Truths add nothing to the roll. They decide whether an action is possible, certain or needs something first (§8).

  • Tags add nothing to the roll. A relevant tag supplies Power instead (§2.5).

Use at most one helpful and one harmful modifier from each of the two slots; equal ratings don’t combine, and the others stay recorded for other actions. Assess each actor separately in a versus test, and don’t add an extra modifier just because the opponent is hindered.

Fixed modifiers. A rule that names a fixed modifier, such as Impede (−3, §10.6) or the Grind’s sick (−1, Module B.3), applies it in addition to the slots; it isn’t a status or Condition and doesn’t use a slot. Adversary Guard raises the Difficulty instead (§10.5), and Gain Position adds a bonus die (§2.4). (Playtest Value)

Example: dice 16 + Skill 3 − exhausted-3 + rehearsed plan 2 − slippery bridge 3 = 15.

Why 2d12 summed: it produces a bell curve (2–24, average 13) — predictable for skilled characters, swingy at the extremes, and big enough that +1 modifiers matter without dominating.

Effort. You can use Effort up to your Effort Level times per roll (1 at start; advancement unlocks 2 and 3). Each use buys one of:

  • +1 bonus die — declared before rolling.

  • +1 Power — declared after a Success or Success at a Cost. It cannot turn a failure into a success.

Example: Effort Level 2 lets you buy one bonus die before the roll, then spend again for +1 Power if you succeed.

Paying costs. Pay Effort and Help in full; you can’t spend points a pool doesn’t have unless a rule says so (§6.1). Paying the last point runs the pool dry; a cost of 0 never does. Pay each listed cost separately, when its rule says. If a cost takes you out, finish that action, but buy no more Effort or abilities for it.

Free benefits. Free bonus dice still count toward the +2 cap. Free Help waives only the pool cost: the helper must still be relevant and still shares the risk. Backing a side at the start of a conflict is not Help (§10.2).

What Woe buys. When you buy a die with Woe, say what you do differently to earn the better chance. If the approach makes extra exposure apparent, the GM describes it; it doesn’t add a second automatic consequence. The Woe is the price, and the GM spends it later (§9.2).

Tag Power. Each relevant tag supplies +1 Power, maximum +2 per action. A tag is relevant only when it materially supports the declared approach; being on your sheet isn’t enough. The same tag can help one action and be compelled in another (§3.4, §4). On a Failure, tags supply nothing, and their Power doesn’t become Weal.

Power buys the result. Spend Power on Effects (§5); the Effects you buy are what the action accomplishes. A successful roll lets you buy them, but doesn’t grant the declared goal by itself. If your purchases meet the requirement agreed before the roll, the goal is achieved; if they fall short, record the progress they make. Any Power you don't spend becomes Weal (party pool, max 6), and accomplishes nothing in the fiction now.

A cost with no Power. With no relevant tags, a Success at a Cost has 0 Power: you buy no Effect, and the cost still applies. Post-roll Effort (§2.4) or another explicit source can add Power. Don’t invent a free status, answer or segment to make up for it.

2.6 Doubles (Twists)

If your two kept dice show the same number:

  • On a success: Critical — +1 Power and add a narrative flourish.

  • On a failure or cost: Complication — the GM introduces a complication right away.

  • Double 12s: an independent test always succeeds, with +2 Power instead of +1.

  • Double 1s: always bring a complication, even on a success, but don’t fail automatically. A success still gains the +1 critical Power.

  • Buying it off. When the dice give you a complication (a twist, or a Risky roll, §2.7), you may cancel it by giving the GM 2 Woe: a problem now, or a problem later. If you keep it and the GM can’t think of a fitting complication, the GM gains the 2 Woe instead.

  • NPCs. When the dice give an NPC a complication, the GM may spend 2 Woe to cancel it. Otherwise the GM describes the complication, or invites the players to.

Base chance of doubles on 2d12 is about 8% (1 in 12). Doubles created by an ability count as rolled doubles. For doubles in versus tests and conflicts, see §10.7.

2.7 Risky Rolls (Optional)

Some rolls aren’t harder, just more dangerous. Before a roll, the GM may call it Risky 1, 2 or 3.

  • Risk die. Roll one extra d12 of a different colour with the others. It’s never kept, doesn’t add to the total and doesn’t count toward the bonus dice maximum.

  • Complication. If the Risk die shows the rating or lower, a complication occurs after the roll is resolved, whatever the result and on top of any twist. You can buy it off for 2 Woe (§2.6).

  • Raising the stakes. The GM may spend 1 Woe to make a roll Risky 1, or to raise its rating by 1 (maximum 3). Settings may make some hazards Risky by default.

3. Character Creation

Character Creation at a Glance

Before creating characters, agree on the campaign premise: the situation that brings you together, the immediate problem, the kinds of characters that fit, and why you will cooperate. For example: isolated settlements must reopen a trade route before winter while rival leaders compete to control it.

These steps describe the order of creation; the subsections below group rules by subject for reference. Revisit earlier choices as your character takes shape.

  1. Establish the campaign premise together.

  2. Sketch your character concept: a role, an important experience, and something you want to explore.

  3. Write your World Belief (§3.5).

  4. Write your Self Belief (§3.5).

  5. Choose Descriptor, Playbook, and Focus (§3.1); complete pools, Edge, skills, tags, abilities, and starting resources (§§3.2–3.4, §3.6).

  6. Choose one Specialisation and two Knowledge Skills (§3.3), and name a starting circle and contact (§3.6).

  7. Introduce the characters and agree on connections, including the link supplied by your Focus.

  8. Write your Other Belief with the other player’s agreement (§3.5).

  9. Choose a concrete starting Goal (§3.5).

  10. Write your Instinct (§3.5).

  11. Review the character with the group.

Specialisations

A Specialisation names a narrow application of a rated skill: write it as Skill (Specialisation), such as Medicine (Trauma Care), Craft (Precision Mechanisms) or Persuade (Mediation).

  • Benefit. When a test uses that skill and the Specialisation directly applies to your approach, add one free bonus die (§2.4). It counts toward the +2 cap. Only one Specialisation applies per test. At the cap it gives no substitute benefit.

  • Approach, not scenery. It applies to how you tackle the problem, not to what is present. Persuade (Mediation) applies when brokering terms between rivals, not when bluffing one of them just because a dispute is under way.

  • Narrow. It must be narrower than its skill. If it would apply to almost every use of the skill, narrow it.

  • Limits. It grants no flat modifier, free Power or extra action, and doesn’t let you substitute one skill for another in a scripted conflict (§10). It combines with Effort, Help or a die bought with Woe, within the cap.

  • Getting one. Start with one. An Advance can buy another (§12.2).

Knowledge Skills

A Knowledge Skill is an unrated field of expertise: Regional Trade Customs, River Ecology, Institutional Records. Start with two, covering subjects your background and skills don’t already cover.

What also counts. Relevant tags from your background count as knowledge within their scope, and so does any skill rated +2 or higher (professional). Don’t take Medicine again as knowledge if you already practise it at +2.

What knowledge does. It adds no die or modifier by itself. It tells you what the character knows, what they can attempt, and what a result means to them:

  1. Routine facts are free. If someone with this expertise would know the answer and it’s available to them, the GM says so. Don’t roll to recall standard practice.

  2. Expertise grants permission (§2.2). It may let the character interpret a technical record or attempt a specialist procedure. If the action is still uncertain and matters, roll the relevant skill.

  3. An expert reads more into the same result. Anyone might find the altered record; an expert also recognises which entry couldn’t come from the normal process. That reading is information, not extra Power.

  4. No single gate. Don’t make one Knowledge Skill the only route to an essential clue. Others can consult a source, find a contact or investigate another way.

Example: a medic recognises without a roll that a patient’s symptoms don’t match the recorded injury. Stabilising the patient during an evacuation is uncertain, so they roll Medicine and add a die for Trauma Care. Knowledge changes what the character understands; a Specialisation changes the roll.

3.4 Tags

A new character has seven character tags: 2 from the Descriptor, 2 from the Playbook and 3 from the Focus.

  • One description, two sides. Each tag is a single description (ex-navy marksman, grandfather’s revolver, owes the wrong people, loyal to the crew). It isn’t split into a benefit and a drawback; what it does depends on the situation.

  • When it helps, it supplies +1 Power on a Success or Success at a Cost, max +2 per action (§2.5). Explain how it helps. Tags never add to or subtract from the roll.

  • When it causes trouble, it can be compelled for a Tychē Point (§4). A tag can supply Power and be compelled in the same action when the help and the trouble are distinct.

  • Burning a tag: mark a relevant tag as burned to gain an automatic +1 Power on a success. It stays unusable until you recover it (a downtime action or a milestone). Burning ignores the +2 cap.

With a +2 cap, a third relevant tag is best used for burning or as Tychē Point invoke material.

3.5 Beliefs, Goals, and Instincts

Three Different Jobs

  • Beliefs state convictions and commitments: why you act. Write one World, one Other, and one Self Belief.

  • Goal states a concrete objective for this session or mission: what you want to accomplish next.

  • Instinct states an if/then reflex: how you habitually respond.

Rewrite a Goal when it’s accomplished or no longer fits, and check it at the start of each session. Beliefs and Instincts can change at milestones (§12.3). Beliefs can justify compels (§4) and meaningful decisions can earn XP (§12.1). They do not create an additional resource.

Writing Your Beliefs

Each Belief combines a conviction with a commitment to act: “I believe this; therefore, I will do this.” Write what you intend to do, not an outcome you are guaranteed to achieve. Avoid a virtue without an action: “I value loyalty” gives less direction than “Those who stand beside me deserve my loyalty; I will not abandon a companion to save myself.”

World Belief

Connect yourself to an important problem, faction, institution, or conflict in the campaign premise. Explain why you cannot simply walk away.

  • What must change, or be protected?

  • Why does it matter to you personally?

  • What will you do about it?

I believe [something about the campaign situation]. Because [why it matters to me], I will [an action or ongoing commitment].

Example: “The settlements will starve if their leaders keep the roads divided. Because I have seen what isolation costs, I will bring them into a common defence agreement.”

Agree with the group that this commitment engages with the campaign you intend to play. Characters need not share an ideology, but should have reasons to participate together.

Self Belief

State a principle about who you are, who you intend to become, or what you consider right. Show how it affects your choices.

  • What principle is more than a convenience?

  • What kind of person are you determined to become, or never become?

  • What will you do when this conviction becomes difficult to uphold?

I believe [a personal principle]. I will live by this conviction by [a commitment], even when [a meaningful pressure or cost].

Example: “People are not expendable tools. I will not knowingly sacrifice those in my care merely to secure an advantage.”

Your Self Belief need not contradict the others, but their demands may sometimes pull in different directions. Do not resolve those possible dilemmas during creation.

Other Belief

Once the characters have concepts and roles, introduce their World and Self Beliefs. Agree on connections and shared history, then choose another PC whose outlook or choices interest you. Your Focus’s existing link may supply this connection; writing a Belief does not grant an additional Focus benefit.

  • Whom do you trust, admire, distrust, or feel you owe?

  • What do you hope to preserve or change in the relationship?

  • How will you act because of that conviction?

I believe [another PC] is / deserves / needs [my conviction about them]. Because [our connection or why it matters], I will [how I intend to act toward them].

Example: “Mara kept our caravan alive when I lost my nerve. I will support her leadership, but I will speak against her when she treats lives as acceptable losses.”

Agree on the relationship premise with the other player. Your Belief expresses your perspective, not authority over their character’s motives or decisions. An agreed misunderstanding is possible; it does not establish your interpretation as fact.

Connecting Beliefs to Your Character

Use Beliefs as prompts, not prerequisites, when completing your mechanical choices:

  • Descriptor: how does your temperament shape your approach?

  • Playbook: what role and methods do you bring?

  • Focus: what distinctive pursuit supports or complicates your commitments?

  • Skills and abilities: what can you already do, and where does ambition exceed competence?

  • Tags: where does each one help you act, and when does it get you into trouble?

  • Load-out: which gear options and load may support your commitments? Follow §3.6 do not choose every item in advance.

Beliefs grant no extra skills, tags, abilities, equipment, pool points or Tychē, and a conviction isn’t a tag. Wanting to protect others doesn’t mean you’re good at it.

Choose Your Starting Goal

Choose a concrete opening objective that advances at least one Belief. It should have a recognisable completion point.

My current Goal is to [achieve a specific result]. I will know it is accomplished when [observable outcome].

Example: “Secure the mill town’s agreement to send a delegate to the road council.”

Completing this Goal does not complete the World Belief about uniting the settlements. A Belief can motivate several successive Goals. Your Goal can instead arise from an Other or Self Belief; it need not serve all three.

Write Your Instinct

Write a recognisable trigger and habitual response. Your Descriptor’s suggested Instinct can be a starting point.

When [trigger], I [habitual response].

Example: “When we enter an unfamiliar place, I identify an escape route before settling in.”

Acting on an Instinct takes no time, but it doesn’t guarantee success or skip a roll the normal rules require. Scripted conflicts limit it further (§10.18).

Review Before Play

Read your three Beliefs, Goal, and Instinct aloud. Check that:

  • Each Belief contains a conviction and intended action.

  • The World Belief connects to the agreed premise.

  • The Other Belief concerns a PC and has their player’s agreement.

  • The Self Belief can influence a difficult choice.

  • The Goal gives you something concrete to pursue immediately.

  • The Instinct has a recognisable trigger.

  • You have reasons to cooperate even when convictions differ.

Using Beliefs to Prepare Play
For each character, note an opportunity and a possible dilemma suggested by their Beliefs. These are invitations, not predetermined outcomes. Challenge commitments without prescribing the correct response. Use Other Beliefs to invite interaction, not override another player’s agency. A character’s conviction does not automatically establish facts about the world. Do not demand a roll merely to prove that the character sincerely holds it.

Example: A Character Ready for Play

Premise: isolated settlements must reopen their trade route before winter.

Concept: a former caravan guard seeking to prevent another disastrous winter expedition. The player completes the normal mechanical choices for protection, travel, or negotiation; these Beliefs do not dictate a single build.

Their World, Other and Self Beliefs, Goal and Instinct are the examples shown above.

The GM might offer a delegation in exchange for escorting a vulnerable supply caravan through a dangerous pass. The Goal provides direction, capabilities determine options, and the Beliefs make decisions matter. The GM does not predetermine the character’s answer.

3.6 Starting Resources

  • Tychē Points: start each session at your Refresh (base 3; §4.1). Beliefs and connections grant no extra points.

  • Starting abilities. You get 1 playbook ability and your Tier 1 Focus ability for free, and may buy up to 2 more playbook abilities at 1 Refresh each.

  • ★ (Major) abilities cost 1 extra Refresh whenever you take them (Generic Playbooks §1.2). Refresh can’t drop below 1 (§4.1).

  • Load-out: choose light, normal or heavy load before the mission, then declare playbook-kit items as you need them and mark their load; don’t buy a full item list in advance. Signature items are chosen at creation and count against load when carried; always-carried items and items marked (0) follow their listed rules (Generic Playbooks §2.1). In conflicts, declare active gear before scripting each exchange (§10.10).

  • Circle and contact. Name one social circle (Dock Workers, The Archive Staff) and one named contact in it or elsewhere. Record both as contact Truths. The circle answers who here might help? the contact answers whom can I ask, and what might it cost them?

Using contacts. Routine help that fits the relationship needs no roll. When access, discretion or cost is uncertain, roll an existing skill such as Persuade or Empathy; there is no separate Network skill or loyalty track. On a Success at a Cost, you get the introduction, shelter or supplies, but owe a favour or expose the relationship. A favour owed uses Module E.7 when that module is in play. A starting contact grants no free Discover, Help or turn; abilities such as Contacts and Network add those benefits.

Ordinary gear, borrowed or bought locally. Getting an ordinary item you didn’t bring is a contact or circle question, not a separate inventory. Load covers what you brought; a contact covers what you can find now; an optional flashback covers what you arranged earlier (§4.2).

4. Tychē Points

Spend 1 Tychē Point to:

  • Invoke — reroll all dice, or add +2 after rolling. This works with any tag or Truth you can justify, even one belonging to the scene or an enemy; the approach must already be possible. A Truth gives no modifier itself, so the benefit comes from the Tychē Point. The +2 sits outside the status and Condition slots and isn’t Tag Power; a Condition’s underlying fact can justify an Invoke, but its rating adds no further free bonus. Invoke before the roll’s outcome is resolved. A reroll changes the dice, not a cost already paid or a consequence already resolved.

  • Declare — establish a minor fact about the scene. The GM may require it to tie to one of your tags.

  • Resist harder — reduce one Resistance cost to 0 (§6).

Declarations stay minor. A declared fact can make an approach possible or supply a relevant asset. It can’t resolve an action by itself, buy status tiers, Situational Condition ratings or Disposition, inflict harm, or change Truths above scene scale (§8). A consequential Create can’t be relabelled minor to avoid its cost.

Resisting harder. The Resistance still resolves normally, but its pool cost is 0, so it can’t run the pool dry, even if the pool is already empty (§6.1). It doesn’t restore spent points or clear an existing running-dry status, and it can’t Resist ordinary Disposition loss or a protected payment (§10.7).

Scripted conflicts add timing limits to all three spends (§10.18).

Earn Tychē Points by:

  • Accepting a compel — the GM or another player offers a meaningful complication based on any of your tags or Scars, Beliefs (§3.5) or your Instinct. Accept → +1 Tychē Point. Refuse → pay 1 Tychē Point.

  • Compelling yourself — propose the same kind of complication on yourself. If the GM agrees it meets the rules below, take the complication and gain +1 Tychē Point. Facing it can also earn XP (§12.1).

What makes a compel

  • Meaningful. The complication must change the situation: an obstacle, obligation, exposure, hard choice, or lost opportunity. Flavour or a minor inconvenience doesn’t count.

  • Clear offer. Before you accept, know which tag, Belief, or Instinct justifies it, what will happen, and what it commits your character to. You otherwise control how your character responds.

  • One tag, two sides. The tag that supplies Power to one action can justify a compel in another, or in the same action when the help and the trouble are distinct. Accepting the compel doesn’t remove the tag’s Power.

  • Limits. A compel must follow from the established meaning of the tag, Belief, or Instinct. It adds no numerical penalty and can’t undo a resolved success. An existing penalty doesn’t earn Tychē just because a tag, Belief, or Instinct explains it.

  • Feared duellist: a rival challenges you before an audience whose support you need. Devoted healer: you stay to treat a dying enemy and lose the chance to pursue their commander.

Beliefs and Compels

All three Belief categories use the same compel procedure. Acting in line with a Belief earns nothing by itself; only a complication that meets the standard above does. Several Beliefs behind one complication still earn one Tychē Point. Beliefs grant no modifiers, are not extra tags and supply no Power.

Belief example: your Self Belief rejects sacrificing people for advantage. You propose staying to escort stranded civilians instead of pursuing the fleeing saboteur. Before acceptance, agree that the delay will let the saboteur escape the immediate pursuit. Accepting that meaningful complication earns 1 Tychē Point; it does not dictate every action you take afterward.

Acting against a Belief is not refusing a compel and carries no automatic penalty (§12.3). An Other Belief never grants control over another PC.

4.1 Refresh

  • Base Refresh is 3. At the start of each session, your Tychē Points reset to your Refresh (if you have more, keep them, up to the cap).

  • Tychē cap. You can hold at most Refresh + 3 Tychē Points. Any you would gain above the cap are lost.

  • Spending Refresh. Creation extras and ★ abilities cost Refresh (§3.6).

  • An attuned artifact (Module A) costs 1 Refresh while attuned, not merely while carried.

  • Minimum Refresh is 1. Purchases can’t take Refresh below 1. Your current Tychē Points can still drop to 0.

  • The advancement option "+1 Refresh" (§12) lets you buy Refresh back, up to the Refresh cap (§12.2).

Refresh happens only at the start of a session, not at a new scene, exchange, Inquiry phase or downtime stay. It restores Tychē Points only; pools, injuries, injury trades and Disposition use their own procedures.

Design intent: power comes at the cost of narrative flexibility. A low-Refresh character is mechanically strong but has to accept compels to stay fuelled.

4.2 Prepared in Advance (Optional)

Once per session, you may spend 1 Tychē Point to establish one plausible thing you prepared earlier: I asked the ferryman to wait after dusk.

  • Obvious or reasonable only. The preparation must be something the character could plainly have done with the time and means they had. A stretch or a wild claim needs the Furcifer’s ★ Flashback (Generic Playbooks).

  • Roll only if it was uncertain. If the earlier action would have needed a roll that matters, make it now, using the normal rules.

  • No rewriting. It can’t contradict an established fact, erase a resolved consequence, or reverse a cost already paid. The ferry we just watched burn was a decoy isn’t allowed.

  • Declaration limits apply. Like Declare, it can’t resolve an action by itself, buy status tiers, Condition ratings or Disposition, change Truths above scene scale, or recalculate Superiority after setup. In scripted conflicts, §10.18 sets its timing.

Load covers what you brought; contacts cover what you can find now (§3.6); a flashback covers what you arranged earlier.

5.1 Personal Statuses

A status is a physical, mental or emotional state of a person (or of a group that shares one, §5.2): wounded, inspired, terrified. External circumstances are Situational Conditions (§5.2), and established facts are Truths (§8).

  • Statuses have tiers 1–6. On a roll, add the tier of the highest relevant helpful status and subtract the tier of the highest relevant harmful status (§2.1).

  • Recording a status. When you create a status, record its name and tier, its scope (who or what it affects, and in which circumstances), and its removal condition (the event, recovery, treatment, or change that ends it), in the format above. The scope must follow from the fiction. Injured ankle — personal status 2 — running, jumping and balancing on the injured leg — ends through appropriate treatment and recovery.

  • What it hinders. A status modifies only rolls within its scope: slashed thigh-3 hinders running and climbing, not reading a ledger. A status can also decide what is possible: no roll lets you use a hand you no longer have. If circumstances change, review its scope; don’t broaden it just because its name allows another reading.

  • Stacking. New harm of the same kind sets the status to one tier above the higher of the existing and incoming tiers (wounded-2 hit by wounded-3 becomes wounded-4). Harm is the same kind when it worsens the same injury, affliction or condition, whatever it’s called: another cut worsens wounded, renewed menace worsens terrified, but a fresh burn needn’t stack with a concussion. The GM says whether new harm stacks before you decide to Resist or accept an injury (§10.11).

  • Order. Resistance and armour reduce incoming harm before it stacks; they can’t reduce a protected payment such as an accepted injury trade (§10.7). Harm reduced to tier 0 isn’t recorded. A status can’t go above tier 6.

  • Tier 6: the target is Taken Out of whatever that status represents (dead, unconscious, captured, convinced, routed), within its scope. A harmful status reaching tier 6 also gives a Scar unless a specific rule replaces it (§6.3). Death needs a lethal cause and established stakes; a physical status at tier 6 can make you Dying (§6.7).

  • Impede is not a status. It is a temporary conflict penalty (§10.6). An ongoing hindrance needs its own name, tier, scope and removal condition.

5.2 Situational Conditions

A Situational Condition is an external circumstance that helps or hinders particular actions by a number: terrain, visibility, positioning, preparation, resources, or other people’s responses (slippery bridge, rehearsed escape plan, supporting witnesses).

  • Ratings 1–6. On a roll, add the rating of the highest relevant helpful Situational Condition and subtract the rating of the highest relevant harmful one (§2.1). The same Condition can help one action and hinder another: thick smoke hinders aiming through it and helps hiding in it.

  • Recording. Use the standard format: rehearsed escape plan — Situational Condition 2 — helps participants follow the agreed routes, signals and fallbacks — ends when the escape is completed or circumstances invalidate the plan.

  • Creating and reducing. Create buys a Situational Condition at 2 Power per rating, and Weaken reduces one at 2 Power per rating by addressing its cause (§5). Established circumstances, equipment, hazards, consequences and explicit abilities can also supply one through their own rules. Weal and Woe can’t buy ratings directly (§9).

  • Stacking. Building on the same circumstance sets the Condition to one rating above the higher of the existing and new ratings, never above 6 (dim lighting 3 darkened by a rating-2 Condition becomes dim lighting 4). This matches status stacking (§5.1).

  • Rating 6 is the most a Condition can be. When a harmful Condition first reaches rating 6, the GM gets one free consequence that fits it (the guards are called, a contact cuts ties) or a matching Truth that prohibits or requires something (discredited 6: nobody here will vouch for you), whichever is more narratively appropriate, with no Woe spent. Choose a consequence as in §2.5 and keep either within the stakes; a Truth created this way lasts as long as the Condition. The Condition doesn’t Take Out, cause a Scar or inflict harm by itself; a dangerous place uses its listed Danger or harm procedure (§8.5, §6).

  • Set the category by cause. Classify by what caused the fact, not by its name, where it’s written or the most useful slot. Renaming doesn’t change it; review category and scope only when circumstances genuinely change.

  5. At tier 6, apply the status’s scope, then record one Scar (§6.3).

  6. Recover the pool and the status separately (§6.5).

6.3 Tier 6 and Scars

When a harmful status reaches tier 6, whether in one hit or through stacking, apply this general procedure unless a specific rule expressly replaces its breaking-point outcome:

  1. Resolve its tier-6 outcome (§5.1).

  2. Apply removal within the status’s scope (below).

  3. Record one Scar, subject to the limits below.

Specific replacements. Some creature tracks and setting conditions state their own breaking-point outcome; use it only where stated (§10.14). A setting condition that replaces the Scar says so; one that doesn’t keeps it. The Scar limits below still apply.

Scope. If the status leaves you unable to pursue your side’s intent, you are Taken Out (§6.4). If it stops only one activity (bound stops you moving, not talking), apply that restriction and stay in the conflict. Set a non-damage status’s scope when it is inflicted, and don’t widen it at tier 6. Either way, the general procedure still gives its Scar.

Not lethal by default. A running-dry status at tier 6 is not deadly on its own. Death needs a lethal cause and established stakes. Tier 6 doesn’t, by itself, cost you a limb, lower a maximum pool or impose any other permanent loss; those need an established consequence, a specific effect or an agreement.

The Scar. The status records your current condition; the Scar records the lasting mark of reaching the breaking point. The player and GM agree on a Scar that fits what happened. A Scar can’t introduce an unrelated injury, dictate an unrelated belief, or extend the opposition’s victory beyond the stakes. Label it with the pool most tied to the condition (a Pneuma Scar):

6.4 Being Taken Out

Ways out. You are Taken Out of a conflict when a harmful status reaches tier 6 within its scope (§6.3), when an effect says so, when you voluntarily withdraw, or when an established circumstance prevents you from pursuing your side’s objective (§10.9). Ordinary Disposition loss never takes an individual out. Once you are out, you can’t start another action, spend Effort or activate an ability unless its rules let you act while Taken Out.

Disposition stays with the side. Being Taken Out doesn’t directly change your side’s shared Disposition. Your side loses your skills, abilities and help, and if none of its participants can continue pursuing its objective, it loses after independent actions finish (§10.9, §10.12).

Out isn’t down. Being Taken Out doesn’t, on its own, make you unconscious, injured or dying. Describe it to fit the fiction: pinned down, disarmed, driven back, cut off, or too frightened to go on. Injury needs a status, death needs established lethal stakes (§6.7), and permanent harm needs an established consequence (§6.8).

Resisting while out. Being Taken Out doesn’t automatically stop you Resisting, but the Resistance must be something you can still do. You can brace against pain while pinned down, but you can’t dodge while fully bound, or use an act of will while unconscious. When a status first takes you out, finish any Resistance already under way. Once a conflict is settled, you can only Resist the consequences that were left open to it; you can’t use Resistance to reopen the intent or delay settlement.

Getting back in. To rejoin a conflict that is still going, and that your side hasn’t lost, you need both of these:

  1. the condition that stopped you must be removed; and

  2. an ability or rule must explicitly let a Taken Out character return.

Returning doesn’t restore any of your side’s Disposition unless the ability says so.

An ordinary Defend can’t bring back a Taken Out ally unless its rules say it can. If you recover after the conflict is over, you act normally in the scenes that follow, subject to your remaining statuses.

  • Affliction (supernatural or ongoing cause): recovers only as its source allows.

Resting off depletion (Playtest Value). When you take one of the 1st–3rd rests, you can put the recovery roll toward the pool, toward its depletion status, or split it between them. Abilities that explicitly restore exhaustion or harmful statuses work as written.

No shortcut. A pool at 0 comes back through these rests like any other. Running dry never gives a special recovery roll, an automatic refill, or a way around the rest cycle. You can’t overrun a pool on purpose to replace a bad recovery roll or to jump to a fresh rest stage.

Each layer recovers on its own. Disposition, pools and statuses each recover only through their own procedure:

  • Disposition is set fresh when a genuinely new conflict begins (§10.2). Fresh Disposition doesn’t refill pools or clear statuses, and a brief pause in the same struggle isn’t a new conflict.

  • Pools recover through the rests above. A full pool doesn’t heal an injury, and an injury doesn’t stop pool recovery unless its rule says so.

  • Statuses recover by their kind: a temporary state ends when its cause or duration ends; injuries need treatment and time (§6.6); afflictions follow their source.

Winning a conflict, ending a scene or setting fresh Disposition is not a general reset.

A skill doesn’t supply the tools, knowledge or fictional permission on its own.

Routine care. In safe conditions, when failure would add nothing, care needs no roll. It gives permission to treat, stabilises where nothing is uncertain, and meets the conditions for natural recovery. It generates no Power. If ordinary treatment could remove one tier of a lasting injury and success is routine, remove that tier and mark the injury treated there’s no surplus Power or Weal. Roll only when an uncertain result matters, never just to farm Weal.

Emergency care stops an injury getting worse: stopping bleeding, splinting a limb, pulling someone away from a harmful influence, isolating a damaged power cell. It doesn’t normally reduce the tier. When the outcome is uncertain, roll the treatment skill vs. Standard 15, with relevant statuses and Situational Conditions modifying the roll. On a success, the deterioration stops or treatment can begin. On a Success at a Cost, the same, with the normal cost. On a failure, the condition is still untreated; the GM announces any further consequence before another attempt. During scripted conflicts, use the conflict-specific consequence restrictions (§10.7).

Restore on lasting injuries. Restore costs 1 Power per tier (§5). It can clear a temporary condition outright when the action removes its cause: putting out burning clothes reduces on fire, calming an ally reduces fear. Lasting injuries are slower:

  • Ordinary treatment reduces a given lasting injury by at most 1 tier. Mark it treated.

  • Further ordinary treatment can’t reduce it again until a healing interval (below) actually removes a tier from it. Interrupting rest, changing healers, renaming the injury or starting a new scene doesn’t clear the mark, and new harm to the same injury doesn’t refresh it. A separate injury has its own mark.

  • Spare Power buys other fitting Effects or becomes Weal as normal.

  • Abilities, cyphers and items that explicitly heal a stated number of tiers (such as healing gel) follow their own text. Abilities that only change the cost of Restore don’t lift the 1-tier limit.

Care in a conflict. Treating someone under opposition uses your action. Emergency care can be a Defend only where the conflict type or an ability allows its skill (such as Battlefield Surgeon). Restoring Disposition keeps your side in the contest; it doesn’t heal an injury unless the action’s rules also provide a healing effect. Healing an injury does not return a Taken Out ally unless the return requirements in §6.4 are met.

  • A brief interruption pauses recovery; it doesn’t erase it. A bad roll on an unrelated action doesn’t worsen an injury; that needs a relevant danger or a stated consequence.

Afflictions (poison, disease, possession, curses, ongoing exposure) don’t follow the interval table. A setting book can give specific afflictions, and creatures that recover in unusual ways, their own rules; where it does, those rules win.

6.7 Dying and Stabilisation

Becoming Dying. After Resistance and stacking, life-threatening physical harm that reaches tier 6 makes a living character Dying, if lethal stakes allow that outcome (§10.2). It also gives a Scar as normal (§6.3), unless an explicit breaking-point replacement applies. The character is incapacitated and can’t take ordinary actions. Start a 3-segment Dying clock. There is no extra dying penalty; the injury already records the harm. Mental defeat, capture, exhaustion and ordinary fear never start a Dying clock, and a running-dry status is never lethal on its own.

Cadence. When Dying begins, set how the clock advances: at the end of each exchange after the one in which Dying began, during a scripted conflict; or after a stated fictional interval, outside one. The clock advances because the interval passes, not because someone attempts treatment. Several characters can act in the same interval without advancing it further. Moving into or out of a scripted conflict carries the existing deadline over; it doesn’t advance the clock or grant extra time.

Further harm. Each further unmitigated physical harm that could worsen the mortal injury marks one extra segment, even though the injury stays at tier 6. One action or hazard can mark at most one extra segment. When the third segment is marked, the character dies.

Stabilising. An adjacent character with suitable means rolls Medicine vs. Hard 18, with relevant statuses and Situational Conditions modifying the roll. An ability or a fitting supernatural method can offer another way.

  • Success: the patient is stable and the clock stops.

  • Success at a Cost: stable, with the normal cost, subject to §10.7 inside conflicts.

  • Failure: still Dying. The attempt uses the action, but doesn’t advance the clock.

Stabilising doesn’t erase marked segments, reduce the injury, refill pools or return the patient to the conflict. A stable patient can be moved with care. If a new event makes them Dying again, the clock resumes from its recorded segments.

Definitive care. Emergency care can stabilise a tier-6 life-threatening injury but can’t lower its tier. Lowering it below tier 6 takes definitive care: a suitable procedure, facility or specialist, or an ability or item that explicitly heals the injury (such as Miracle Worker, Not on My Table or healing gel). The GM states what that requires when the injury is assessed and doesn’t add requirements once they are met. When definitive care brings the injury below tier 6 and removes its immediate lethal cause, clear the Dying clock.

Immediate death. Use it only when the established outcome leaves no plausible time for rescue, or a specific killing method calls for it (such as a creature’s finishing procedure in a setting book). Announce that danger before the roll, and allow Resistance, escape or interruption where their rules permit it; accepted payments cannot be cancelled through these general options. Once such an outcome is fully resolved, don’t add a Dying clock to undo it; equally, don’t skip the clock for an ordinary mortal wound just because the description is graphic. Resolve permitted post-conflict finishing procedures before compromise (§10.12).

Setting-specific harm. Use a condition’s specific tiers, treatment and breaking-point rules where stated. Replace the core Dying clock only if its rule explicitly supplies an alternative death or stabilisation procedure; different condition tiers alone do not replace it. A condition with its own tiers but no death procedure still uses the core Dying clock when it reaches a lethal tier 6. Never run a second death deadline alongside it.

After a death. Resolve the death and its established consequences. The group may remember the character through Beliefs, relationships, keepsakes or later story developments; death creates no separate points or automatic Truth.

6.8 Permanent Harm

Where it comes from. An injury doesn’t become permanent just because its tier is high. Permanent harm needs an established permanent-injury stake, a specific effect or creature rule, a voluntary bargain, or a Scar-giving event. Reaching tier 6 gives a Scar (§6.3), not a permanent loss.

The wound heals; the loss stays. A permanent change can outlast the injury that caused it. When severed hand-4 has healed, the hand is still gone: the status measured the acute wound. Record the lasting change as a tag or a character fact, not as a status that never heals.

Adapting. Training, help, prostheses, advanced technology or supernatural intervention can change how permanent harm affects play. A replacement doesn’t automatically remove a related Scar, and a Scar doesn’t make a replacement useless. Don’t reduce maximum pools, Edge, skills or Refresh unless the effect or an agreed Advance says so.

7. Progress Clocks

  • Sizes: 4 (simple), 6 (complex), 8 (daunting), 12 (epic). Specific rules may prescribe other sizes, such as the 16-segment Region Truth clock (Module C.6) or the 3-segment Dying clock (§6.7).

  • Player clocks tick with the Advance effect (1 Power = 1 segment).

  • Danger clocks tick when the GM spends Woe (1 Woe = 1 segment) or when an established failure consequence or explicit procedure advances them.

  • Racing clocks: two clocks run together (e.g. Crack the vault 8 vs. Guards arrive 6).

  • Linked clocks: filling one opens or unlocks another.

  • Downtime projects normally use 8- or 12-segment clocks; a specific ability or project may prescribe another size, including 6 segments.

Specific procedures govern. A clock’s own rule sets its triggers, pace and permitted spends, and the general lines above don’t override it. Examples: the Grind (Module B) and the Dying clock (§6.7).

Clocks are not Disposition. A clock measures its named goal; Disposition measures a side’s ability to pursue a conflict (§10.18).

8. Truths

A Truth is an established fact about a person, group, object, place or the world. It can permit or prevent an approach, set a requirement, establish a danger or define the setting.

Truths have no rank and no modifier. A Truth doesn’t change rolls, Difficulty, Power, status tiers, Situational Condition ratings or Disposition by itself. When a circumstance should help or hinder a roll by a number, record it as a Situational Condition (§5.2). One circumstance can be both: Barred River Gate is a Truth; thick smoke 3 is a Situational Condition.

  • Permission: a drainage tunnel reaches the inner courtyard.

  • Prohibition: the door has no keyhole.

  • Requirement: admission requires the royal seal.

  • Danger: flame can ignite the gas in the cellar.

  • World fact: magic is real but feared.

8.1 Possibility Before Difficulty

Check relevant Truths before anyone rolls (§2.2):

  • Possible and uncertain: roll normally.

  • Prevented: explain the restriction. The character changes the circumstances or the approach.

  • Certain: it happens without a roll.

  • Requirement unmet: establish how it is met or bypassed first.

No bonus overcomes an impossible approach. A Truth restricts only what its wording and scope support: a barred gate doesn’t make the hinges unbreakable or the wall unclimbable. Use a Situational Condition for numerical interference and a Truth for a categorical limit. Poor visibility can hinder observation; complete darkness can rule out ordinary visual identification. Don’t penalise an impossible attempt instead of explaining why it can’t work.

8.3 Establishing and Recording

Truths come from agreed setup, actions, discovery, explicit abilities or purchases, and binding consequences or concessions. Recording a fact that already exists costs nothing.

  • Creating Truths: players use the Create effect (2 Power, §5) or spend 2 Weal the GM spends 2 Woe (§9). A created Truth is scene scale and lasts for the scene; only Create can pay +1 Power to make it outlast the scene (§5). It grants no Situational Condition rating.

  • State it first. Say what fact you intend to create before its action resolves, and agree its scope, requirements, cost and consequences. A Truth can’t contradict resolved events, exceed the action’s reach, grant an unrelated objective, decide another character’s choices without their player’s agreement, or widen agreed stakes.

  • Recording a Truth. Use the standard format (§5): Name — Truth/scale — scope and what it implies — ending condition. It applies only within its scope, and a removal condition never extends a duration. Barred River Gate — Truth/Scene — the gate can’t open normally until its inner bar is lifted, broken or bypassed — ends when the bar no longer secures it.

8.4 Changing and Ending Truths

  • Change the fact. Lift the bar, disperse the gas, repair the bridge, obtain the seal. Use no roll, a test, a conflict or a project, whichever fits. Truths aren’t worn down rank by rank; track partial progress with a clock, a Situational Condition or a revised fact.

  • Duration. Established facts last until something changes them. A created Truth also follows its creating rule’s duration and ending condition.

  • Expiry doesn’t rewind. When a Truth ends, describe why: the passage closes, support is withdrawn, the authorisation lapses. Its end never reverses a completed action or erases a consequence; a crossing already made isn’t undone when the route closes. Removing a cause doesn’t heal a separate injury.

8.5 Dangers and Conflicts

  • Dangerous circumstances. A circumstance is dangerous when it carries established lethal stakes or a listed Danger (Gas Leak, Collapsing Floor §10.8), not because of a rating. It can be a Truth, a Situational Condition or both. Record its scope, what exposes someone to it, its consequence, and how it can be avoided, Resisted or mitigated. Woe from dangerous circumstances is capped per scene (§9.2).

  • In conflicts, Truths set approaches and scenery. They don’t by themselves cancel scripts, grant turns, change Disposition, take participants out, override action interactions or settle a disputed objective; only an explicit ability can. If changing a Truth could settle the conflict, establish that before the deciding action resolves. Conflicts add further limits on creating Truths (§10.18).

Invoking a Truth. A Truth carries no modifier, but it can still justify a Tychē Invoke (§4).

10. Conflict System

Use a scripted conflict for a consequential struggle in which opposing sides can make several meaningful tactical decisions. A conflict might be a fight, chase, argument, negotiation, infiltration, battle, supernatural contest, or struggle against a dangerous environment.

Each side declares an intent, establishes its starting Disposition, and secretly chooses three actions. Reveal and resolve those actions one volley at a time. Continue until one side wins, both sides are defeated, or the participants agree to a concession.

Disposition measures a side’s ability to keep pursuing its objective. It isn’t health, ammunition, morale alone, or a count of individual participants.

10.1 When to Use a Conflict

Use a scripted conflict when all three conditions are present:

  • The outcome matters enough to deserve sustained attention.

  • An opponent or significant force can actively resist the intended outcome.

  • Several meaningful tactical choices could change how the struggle ends.

Otherwise, use an ordinary roll or resolve the situation without rolling. A named opponent, a dangerous situation, or a large group doesn’t automatically require a conflict.

Each Side Needs an Objective

State what each side wants to accomplish, not merely what it wants to prevent. Suitable objectives include:

  • Hold the bridge until the refugees cross.

  • Capture the courier before she reaches the harbour.

  • Convince the council to release the prisoners.

  • Escape the hunters without revealing the refuge.

  • Reach shelter before the storm cuts off the pass.

An objective may be defensive. Holding the bridge until the refugees cross gives the defenders something concrete to achieve. Merely stopping the enemy usually needs more definition.

Environmental opposition also needs a stated outcome: the storm strands the travellers, the fire consumes the archive, or the river carries away the crossing.

10.2 Setup

Complete these steps before scripting:

  1. Declare intent and stakes.

  2. Choose the conflict type.

  3. Establish participants and sides.

  4. Calculate starting Disposition.

  5. Apply Superiority to both sides and resolve any setup defeat (§10.3).

  6. Record the adjusted starting Disposition.

  7. Establish the acting order and script the first exchange.

Intent and Stakes

Each side states what it gains by winning. Establish what losing allows the opposition to do and whether capture, death, permanent injury, exposure, or another lasting consequence is possible.

Discuss what withdrawal or concession could look like.

Intent limits victory. Defeating a side whose objective is to hold a doorway doesn’t automatically let the winner kill everyone, seize unrelated possessions, or achieve another objective.

Lethal Stakes

Establish lethal stakes before the roll or exchange that could produce a lethal outcome.

Stakes may change when circumstances change, but never retroactively. Explain the new danger and allow affected participants to respond before resolving it.

Lethal stakes make death possible, not automatic (§10.12).

Participants

A participant must be able to contribute to the side’s objective. Backing the side at setup commits a character to that side’s stakes. Helping an action during the conflict likewise makes the helper a participant if they were not already involved.

A character who can’t meaningfully contribute may remain outside the conflict. Don’t include them merely to increase Disposition. Companions and groups follow §10.9.

Starting Disposition

The leader normally rolls 2d12 + Disposition skill + highest Edge in that skill’s pool. Divide the result by three, rounding down. Then:

  • Add +1 per eligible backer. Backing isn’t Help: it costs no pool points and gives no bonus die. Before the leader rolls, each backer explains a meaningful contribution and commits to the side’s stakes. A free-Help ability adds nothing more unless it says so.

  • Apply flat starting-Disposition bonuses and penalties from abilities, creature rank, conditions, and similar effects; these have no stacking cap.

  • Apply a minimum of 1.

  • Apply opposing Superiority (§10.3).

Record the adjusted total. It stays fixed for the conflict as the recovery cap and compromise reference, even if participants later leave (§10.9).

Adversary exception. Sides built with Grit use the Adversaries setup procedure instead of the leader’s Disposition roll.

Continuing the Same Conflict

Set fresh Disposition only for a genuinely new conflict with a materially different objective or changed circumstances. Passing through a doorway, changing tactics, or turning a Fight into a Chase doesn’t by itself begin a new conflict. Carry forward:

  • Current and starting Disposition.

  • Used injury trades.

  • Taken Out participants.

  • Existing injuries, Situational Conditions, Truths, and other continuing effects.

If the type changes, establish the new action skills before the next exchange. Don’t reapply setup bonuses merely because the type changes.

10.3 Superiority

Preparation can weaken an opposing side before the conflict begins. Gain 1 Superiority for each applicable category, to a maximum of 3:

  • Know Thy Foe: discover an exploitable weakness, motive, fear, or vulnerability.

  • Know Thy Battlefield: secure an advantageous position or understand circumstances that materially favour your approach.

  • Know Thy Players: establish a useful plan, alliance, resource, or other preparation.

Each category applies once and requires a distinct material advantage affecting the opposing side and the declared stakes. Rephrasing one advantage doesn’t qualify it for several categories.

After all other starting-Disposition modifiers and the minimum of 1, reduce each opposing total by your Superiority, to a minimum of 0. Determine both adjusted totals before resolving the outcome. Superiority isn’t tracked or spent afterward.

Overwhelming Preparation

  • Only one side at 0: its opponent wins at no cost, within the declared intent and stakes. Don’t script exchanges or calculate compromise.

  • Both sides at 0: resolve mutual defeat (§10.12), without scripting or a winner’s compromise. Neither receives an outright or no-cost victory.

  • Neither side at 0: record both totals and proceed to scripting.

Resolve setup defeat as recorded: don’t restore a total to 1, reroll setup, or compare remainders or Superiority to break a tie. Only opposing Superiority can take a total below the minimum of 1.

Setup defeat inflicts no injury, pool loss or Taken Out by itself, and doesn’t satisfy a creature’s finishing requirement. Consequences follow the stakes and the victory or mutual-defeat outcome.

Preparation and Other Benefits

An asset may retain its normal benefits while also contributing to Superiority. A map might justify preparation and later help an action. These benefits perform different jobs:

  • Superiority represents preparation at setup.

  • An action tag supplies Power to a particular action.

  • A Situational Condition modifies relevant rolls; a Truth establishes what is possible.

Apply each only when its requirements are met. One asset doesn’t automatically provide all four.

10.5 Scripting and Volleys

Each exchange contains three volleys.

Each side secretly scripts three actions in order, assigning an eligible participant to each. Both sides commit their scripts before revealing the first volley.

Reveal the corresponding actions together, describe them, and resolve their interaction. Repeat for the second and third volleys unless the conflict ends.

If it continues, script another exchange.

Coordination and Rotation

A side may appoint a coordinator to record its script and acting order. The coordinator doesn’t have exclusive authority over the group’s decisions. Rotation runs in rounds and carries across exchanges. Each round, every available participant acts once, in any order, and nobody acts in two volleys in a row unless they are the only one available. Helping doesn’t count as a turn. Update rotation when someone joins, withdraws, or becomes unable to act. Assigning an action that later proves unsuitable doesn’t permit a free replacement.

An ongoing hindrance needs its own name, tier or rating, scope and removal condition (§5), and Impede and other tactical effects don’t become statuses or Conditions automatically. For example: Entangled — harmful status 2 — movement and actions requiring free limbs — ends when the net is cut or the target escapes. State fictional restrictions directly; a helpless body or inaccessible weapon isn’t shorthand for temporary Impede.

Disarming and Denying Options

Identify the disabled asset and explain how. Disabling one asset removes an action category only if it was the side’s only plausible means of performing that action. A replacement may restore access when the fiction permits. Switching equipment doesn’t itself change a script.

10.7 Costs, Failures and Complications

Use normal Effort, Weal, Woe, Tychē Points, tags, Situational Conditions and Truths subject to these restrictions and the limits in §10.18.

Ordinary Failure

An ordinary failed independent test produces no action effect and generates the usual 1 Woe. It doesn’t also authorise an unrelated GM move, automatic injury or additional Disposition loss: the failed and opposing actions establish the volley.

Losing or tying a versus test, or being trumped, generates no ordinary-failure Woe. Apply this symmetrically to player and GM actors; costs already paid remain spent. Separately triggered complications, ability costs, scenery Dangers and explicit Woe rules still apply.

Success at a Cost

On an independent test missing by 1–3, gain Tag Power + 0 as usual (§2.5). The player chooses either 1 Woe for the GM or an immediate, proportionate complication arising from the action.

An ordinary cost can’t impose an additional status or extra Disposition loss unless an explicit rule permits it. Suitable costs include exposing an approach, losing access to a minor asset or establishing a limited adverse fact.

Scenery actions use their listed Danger when the consequence option is chosen.

Doubles

Doubles work as in §2.6, including doubles created by an ability such as Perfect Strike. In versus tests:

  • Double 12s don’t override a higher opposing total or break a tie.

  • Winning doubles add +1 Power to the margin award (double 12s: +2). Losing or tied doubles grant no Power or effect.

  • Double 1s still bring their complication on a loss or tie, but don’t lose automatically.

Doubles complications stay within the stakes and the action’s scale. They don’t cancel an independent action or change the script. Buy them off normally (§2.6).

Explicit Risks and Harm

Abilities, hazards, Risky rolls, scenery Dangers and other explicit rules may impose their stated consequences. Resolve these separately from ordinary Disposition loss.

Protected Payments

What counts. A protected payment is a consequence accepted in exchange for a benefit: an accepted injury trade (§10.11), a running-dry status from paying a cost (§6.2), an agreed concession cost (§10.12), or any other cost accepted as payment.

What can’t touch it. Resistance, armour, prevention, redirection, immunity and incidental clearing, including a double-12 result, can’t prevent, reduce or remove a protected payment. Only an exception that names the protected payment can change it. Generic wording such as “cancel harm” isn’t enough.

Disposition. Generic wording such as ignore harm, cancel a consequence, take the hit or protect an ally doesn’t affect shared Disposition. A rule must explicitly mention Disposition to reduce, redirect, restore or prevent its loss.

Afterwards. Once recorded, a status can be treated or healed through the applicable procedure. Later healing doesn’t refund the benefit, restore Disposition, refresh an injury trade or erase a Scar.

10.9 Shared Disposition and Participation

Each side has one shared total. Don’t divide it among participants; there are no individual shares or spill-over. Ordinary Disposition loss removes no particular participant.

A participant stops acting when a status, explicit effect, withdrawal or established circumstance prevents contribution to the objective.

A side loses at 0 Disposition or when none of its participants can continue pursuing its objective. Check victory using the action-completion rule in §10.5.

Companions and Groups

Distinguish a participant, which takes assigned actions and is bound by the side’s stakes, from an asset, which supplies tags, information or explicitly permitted helping benefits without independent turns.

Don’t count the same companion twice merely because it can perform both functions.

An independently acting companion occupies a place in continuous rotation, uses its listed skills or ratings and can be prevented from acting or Taken Out. It has no separate Disposition track or injury trade unless explicitly granted one.

A group represented by one rules entry is one participant for rotation and area-effect Disposition bonuses, not one per fictional body. Apply specific Bodies or mass-combat rules only where provided.

Reinforcements and Recovery

Starting Disposition stays fixed for the conflict (§10.2). Reinforcements add their listed Grit to current shared Disposition, even above the starting value. They don’t change starting Disposition or the normal recovery cap, grant additional scripted actions, permit free actor reassignment or recalculate setup.

Defend and other Disposition-recovery effects restore only up to the starting value unless explicitly stated otherwise. At or above the cap, recovery restores nothing and doesn’t reduce the current total.

Removing a participant, backer, companion or group subtracts no contribution or Grit from current or starting Disposition. Removal may still defeat the side if nobody remains able to pursue its objective.

10.12 Defeat, Mutual Defeat and Concession

Apply the action-completion rule in §10.5 before determining the outcome.

Defeat and Finishing Procedures

The winner achieves its intent within the established stakes, subject to compromise. Don’t rerun the same contest; a new conflict needs a materially different objective or changed circumstances.

Defeat doesn’t automatically inflict injury. Where a creature or setting requires a finishing procedure (such as a setting book’s killing strike), victory earns the chance to attempt it: finish the deciding volley, resolve the procedure as its entry describes, then settle compromise.

Mutual Defeat

If both sides are defeated in the same volley, or both start at 0 after Superiority, neither wins outright.

Resolve compatible parts of both intents and a shared setback within the stakes. Neither receives an incompatible outcome automatically.

Calculate no winner’s compromise. Setup defeat follows §10.3.

Concession

Before the next roll, propose an end to the conflict. State what the opponent gains, what your side retains, all injuries, losses, promises or obligations, and how the immediate struggle ends.

Offer meaningful success that fits the situation. A mindless predator may require an escape opportunity or sacrifice rather than surrender.

If accepted, apply the terms without another roll. Don’t also apply compromise unless included. Agreed costs are protected payments (§10.7); injuries recover normally.

If rejected, continue. Conceding grants no automatic Tychē Point.

What Compromise Means

The winner achieves its intent, but the loser earns a proportionate concession:

  • Minor: limited concession, delay, obligation, expenditure or complication.

  • Significant: substantial concession, restricted terms, important loss, serious injury or meaningful part of the loser’s objective.

  • Major: narrow victory at a major price: lasting obligation, severe injury, important loss or powerful new danger.

These are consequence packages, not mandatory lists. Don’t automatically add injury to an adequate non-injury concession.

Compromise can’t simply reverse the result or grant the loser’s complete incompatible objective.

Negotiating Terms

  1. Find the grade from the table above.

  2. Agree one package. The loser proposes it: what the winner achieves, what the loser keeps or gains, and every cost and who pays it.

  3. Settle injuries. Credit eligible existing injuries, then resolve eligible Resistance.

  4. Record the binding result.

Keep terms proportionate, relevant and within the stakes. If agreement proves difficult, the GM adjudicates using these limits. Disagreement doesn’t cancel earned compromise.

Injury Guidance

When appropriate to the package, use tier 2 for Minor, tier 3 for Significant and tier 4 for Major as starting guidance, not automatic injuries, minimum costs or penalties for everyone.

Existing Injuries

An injury suffered during this conflict satisfies one injury cost assigned to that character if its current tier meets or exceeds the required tier. Each injury counts once.

Don’t pool, split, transfer or credit injuries against non-injury consequences. An injury treated below the required tier no longer satisfies the cost.

Add no replacement injury because an existing one satisfies the first.

Resistance

Imposed compromise statuses can be Resisted normally (§6); agreed payments can’t (§10.7). Resisting an injury doesn’t revoke the concession or other stated consequences, and adds no replacement cost.

Binding Results

Victory, concessions, promises and consequences stand. Genuinely new events may change them; don’t ignore or immediately reroll them because someone dislikes the result.

10.14 NPCs and Harm

Ordinary NPCs and minion groups can’t trade injury. A named rival built as a full PC may; others require an explicit trait.

Ordinary NPCs have no pools (§6.1) and reduce imposed harm only through listed Armour, traits or abilities. Apply at most one reduction per imposed status application, never to ordinary Disposition loss or a protected payment (§10.7).

NPC defeat uses §10.12. Capture, escape, injury and death follow the stakes and outcome. Use a Dying clock when rescue matters, not to make an accepted rescue objective impossible.

Creature-specific rules remain in force, including breaking points that replace the tier-6 procedure (§6.3). Immunity may stop an unsuitable status without stopping Disposition loss in a contest the creature can lose.

10.15 Order of Resolution

Setup

  1. Establish objectives, stakes, participants and exits.

  2. Calculate starting Disposition and Superiority.

  3. Determine both adjusted totals and resolve any setup defeat (§10.3).

Each exchange

  1. Script the exchange and assign actors.

Each volley

  1. Reveal the next volley.

  2. Determine the interaction.

  3. Pay pre-roll costs; resolve running dry.

  4. Roll and determine Power.

  5. Make permitted post-roll choices and pay their costs.

  6. Allocate Power.

  7. Calculate Disposition loss separately from statuses.

  8. Offer eligible injury trades.

  9. Apply remaining Disposition loss.

  10. Resist eligible statuses, stack what remains and resolve tier 6, including explicit creature exceptions.

  11. Complete any independent opposing action.

  12. Check removal, victory and mutual defeat.

  13. Before the next roll, allow concession proposals.

Repeat steps 5–17 for each volley, and step 4 for each new exchange, until a side is defeated or concedes.

Ending

  1. Resolve permitted creature finishing procedures before compromise.

  2. Settle compromise or concession.

  3. Recover each layer only through its own procedure (§6.5).

Specific rules override general rules only on the subject addressed.

10.16 Keeping It Fast

  • Use full conflicts only when several tactical decisions matter.

  • Record objectives and starting Disposition visibly.

  • Use three cards or entries for each script.

  • Keep one continuous acting order per side.

  • Treat minion groups as one coordinated participant unless separation matters.

  • Describe volleys briefly; reserve extended narration for decisive moments.

  • Negotiate one coherent compromise package, not accumulating penalties.

Disposition around 8–11 is the initial pacing target. Aim for two or three exchanges and roughly 15–20 minutes, then adjust through playtesting rather than adding steps.

10.17 Area Effects

(Playtest Value) An area effect affects everyone within its established area whom the effect can reach. Establish the area and affected participants before resolving it. An area effect doesn’t automatically distinguish allies from enemies; selective targeting requires an explicit rule or circumstances that keep others outside its reach.

Disposition loss in conflicts. When an area Attack or Feint applies its normal Disposition-loss effect:

  1. Apply the committed Power + 1 to the opposing side’s shared Disposition once.

  2. Add 1 Disposition loss for each additional opposing participant affected beyond the first.

Loss goes to the shared total, never to individuals. A group with one rules entry counts as one participant (§10.9).

Example: an area Attack commits 3 Power to Disposition loss and affects three opposing participants. It removes 6 shared Disposition: 3 Power + 1 for the normal Attack, +2 for the two additional participants.

Apply any eligible injury trade against the combined loss (§10.11). The area Attack remains one Attack: no more than one participant may accept an injury against it.

Explicitly inflicted statuses. When an area effect explicitly inflicts a status, every affected character receives the full purchased or listed tier. Resolve each character’s Resistance or NPC harm reduction separately, then stack the remaining status normally.

Example: an effect that inflicts burned-3 in an area inflicts burned-3 on every affected character, not tier 3 on one and tier 1 on the others.

An ordinary conflict Attack or Feint inflicts no status just because it covers an area; it inflicts both loss and statuses only if an explicit rule says so. Outside conflicts, area effects inflict statuses normally and no Disposition loss. Offensive bonuses apply to their stated component (§10.6).

10.18 Conflict Limits Reference

These limits apply to every scripted conflict. Specific rules override them only on the subject they address.

Core Rules in Conflicts

  • Invoke during an eligible roll, before its outcome is resolved. It changes only that roll: no extra turn, script change or reassigned actor. A trumped action or automatic success has no roll to invoke on.

  • Specialisations add their die only when the action’s permitted skill is the Specialisation’s skill and it applies to the approach (§3.3). They don’t permit a different skill, action or Effect. Knowledge informs choices but doesn’t add an action.

  • Prepared in Advance (§4.2), if used, is a declaration: the next item’s limits apply, and it can’t change a revealed script.

  • Declarations can make an approach possible or supply an asset. They can’t resolve a scripted action, cancel an independent action, widen the stakes or reverse an accepted concession.

  • Compels. Agree the consequence before accepting. A compel must respect the stakes and action timing, and can’t change a script or cancel an independent action. If its terms settle the conflict, use Concession (§10.12) rather than another roll. It can’t erase a protected payment.

  • Instincts. Acting on an Instinct takes no time but grants no extra action, interrupt, free script change, actor reassignment or rotation bypass. A consequential response needs a permitted action or explicit ability; an Instinct can’t rescue a trumped action, allow action while Taken Out or retroactively cancel a resolved effect.

  • Truths and Conditions. Creating a Truth or Situational Condition, or reducing a Condition, takes a permitted action or explicit exception (§10.6); changing a Truth means changing the fact (§8.4); Weal and Woe spends grant no extra turn, free reassignment or retroactive cancellation. A Truth can set up a later scenery Maneuver but doesn’t resolve it or its Danger (§10.8). A hazardous description doesn’t grant both Disposition loss and a harmful status; explicit harm and area effects use their own procedures (§10.17).

  • Clocks and Disposition are separate tracks. Don’t convert loss, healing or clock segments between them without an explicit rule. Advance is available only to actions §10.6 permits.

  • Complications, from dice or bought with Woe, stay within the stakes and timing. They don’t change committed scripts or cancel independent actions unless an explicit rule permits it.

Free, Immediate and Automatic Effects

Free removes only the stated resource cost. It grants no extra turn, rotation bypass or permission to act while Taken Out. A passive benefit or a benefit explicitly requiring no action can apply without consuming a scripted action.

An immediate effect resolves at its stated trigger. It interrupts another action only if explicitly permitted, and can’t retroactively cancel resolved costs or effects.

An additional immediate action must specify its trigger, replacement of scripted actions, rotation treatment, use while Taken Out and interaction with independent actions.

Automatic success replaces the roll for an otherwise permitted action. It supplies no extra action, script change, rescue of a trumped action, expanded stakes or unsuitable approach. In a versus test, an automatic success wins unless both sides automatically succeed; then it’s a tie unless a rule says otherwise.

On an independent test, apply the listed Power without rolling. In a versus test, it wins with its listed Power unless the opposing action also succeeds automatically; then resolve a tie unless a specific rule says otherwise. No roll means no dice-based critical or complication.

12. Advancement

A milestone is the end of a story arc or the completion of a major Goal; the GM calls it, usually at the end of a session. A major milestone is the end of a full campaign arc. (Playtest Value)

12.1 Earning XP (end of session)

1 XP per “yes” unless noted:

  • Did you hit your playbook XP trigger? (1, or 2 in a scene with lethal stakes or a listed Danger, §8.5)

  • Did you accomplish your Goal? (+2)

  • Did one or more of your World, Other, or Self Beliefs drive a meaningful decision?

  • Did your Instinct get you into trouble or save the day?

  • Did you face consequences from a compelled tag or Scar?

The Belief question awards at most 1 XP per session, not 1 XP per Belief. Upholding, questioning, or acting against a Belief can qualify when it drives a meaningful decision; merely reciting or rewriting it does not.

MVP vote: the table votes on one standout moment each session; that player gets +1 Tychē Point at the next session (above Refresh, within the cap).

12.2 Spending XP (every 6 XP = 1 Advance)

  • +1 to a skill (following the pyramid rule)

  • +3 to one pool, or +1 Edge (max 3 Edge per pool)

  • A new playbook special ability (★ costs 1 Refresh; veteran costs below)

  • A new tag, or rewrite an existing tag

  • A new Specialisation in a rated skill (§3.3)

  • +1 Refresh (buys back Refresh spent on abilities or artifacts), up to the Refresh cap of 3 + 1 per 3 Advances

  • A higher-tier Focus ability: Tier 2 after 3 Advances; Tier 3 after 6 Advances. Taking the ability costs an Advance; reaching the threshold does not grant it automatically (Generic Focuses §1.1).

  • +1 Effort Level (to Level 2 after 3 Advances; Level 3 after 9 Advances)

  • Quick Recovery (once only): recover 1 additional point on every recovery roll. It doesn’t change the fourth rest’s full recovery (§6.5).

Focus choices. Tier 1 is gained at creation. At Tiers 2 and 3, choose one of the tier’s two options when buying that ability. Once eligible for the tier, its unchosen option can be taken later as a normal Advance. A ★ option also costs 1 Refresh.

Veteran abilities. After 6 Advances, you may take an ability from another playbook as an Advance. It costs 1 Refresh plus any ★ cost, so a veteran ★ costs 2 Refresh (Generic Playbooks §1.2).

Capstones. A capstone unlocks after 12 Advances if you have at least 4 abilities from that playbook. Take it as an Advance; it costs no Refresh, and a character can have only one.

Refresh limits. Purchases can’t take Refresh below 1 (§4.1) or above the Refresh cap.

12.3 Reviewing Beliefs and Instincts

Review the relevance of your Beliefs at session end. Ask:

Which conviction did events reinforce, complicate, or overturn?

Use the answer to review your World, Other and Self Beliefs. A conviction may stay firm, grow more complicated or stop fitting the character; you don’t have to change a Belief because you questioned or acted against it. The question is a reflection prompt, not a reward: answering it earns nothing by itself (§12.1).

Ordinary revisions happen at milestones. Reaffirm, revise, or replace your Beliefs to reflect development in play.

  • Reaffirm: keep a conviction and commitment that still matter.

  • Revise: update a fulfilled or obsolete action clause while keeping the underlying conviction.

  • Replace: write a new Belief when experience changes your outlook or the former commitment no longer belongs in the campaign.

  • Relationships: review an Other Belief when the relationship meaningfully changes; agree on shared premises with the other player as in §3.5.

Acting against a Belief has no automatic penalty and doesn’t force a rewrite; the player decides whether it leads to reaffirmation or change. Don’t rewrite a Belief mid-decision to claim a reward. Rewriting alone earns neither XP nor Tychē.

Keep one World, one Other and one Self Belief. Rewrite a Goal when it’s accomplished or no longer fits (§3.5). Instincts can change at milestones.

13. Example of Play

Kade, a Reckless Furcifer who Never Leaves Anyone Behind (Mētis Edge 1), is creeping through a warehouse to free a captured friend. He has bought Effort Level 2 with an Advance; new characters start at Level 1 (§2.4, §12.2). The scene has dim lighting — Situational Condition 2 — helps hiding in the shadows, hinders reading and aiming — ends when the lamps are lit or the scene ends, and the Truth Patrolling Guards.

  • Goal: reach the cell unseen. The table agrees that means advancing the 4-segment Reach the Cell clock; each segment costs 1 Power (Advance).

  • Roll: Stealth +3, dim lighting +2. The GM sets Hard 18, so Kade needs 13+ on the dice. His tags quiet boots and knows this district add nothing to the roll but supply 2 Tag Power if he succeeds.

  • Effort, part 1: Kade spends 3 − 1 Edge = 2 Mētis for a bonus die → 3d12 keep 2.

  • Result: dice show 9, 9, 4 → keep 9 + 9 = 18, +5 = 23. That's 5 over → 2 Power, plus 2 Tag Power = 4 Power, plus doubles on a success → +1 = 5 Power.

  • Effort, part 2: Kade spends another 2 Mētis for +1 Power → 6 Power.

  • Spend: Advance the Reach the Cell clock by 4 (4 Power), filling it: he reaches the cell unseen. Discover (1 Power): "Where is the key?" — "On the sergeant's belt." 1 Power left → +1 Weal.

  • Had it cost him: on a Success at a Cost he would have had only his 2 Tag Power: two segments, short of the cell, plus a consequence or 1 Woe.

  • Later: the sergeant's dread patron, a bound spirit, lashes out with terror-3. Kade Resists with Pneuma (Edge 0), so 2d12: highest die 7 → cost 2 Pneuma. The status drops to terror-1.

  • Compel: the GM compels Reckless: "You can't wait for backup; you go in now." Kade accepts and gains +1 Tychē Point.

13.1 A Scripted Conflict

Later, at the warehouse gate, Kade, Mira and Tarin confront two guards. This is a new scene: the earlier Truth and Condition don’t apply, and Kade’s terror was treated once the spirit’s influence ended (§6.6). His spent Mētis and Pneuma stay spent. The PCs want to get their friend through the gate; the guards want to hold them until reinforcements arrive. The stakes are capture and nonlethal injury, not death.

Setup. Mira leads with Fight +3 and the highest Edge in its pool, 1. Her setup dice are 7 and 8: 7 + 8 + 3 + 1 = 19. Divide by 3 and round down to 6, then add +1 for each of her two eligible backers: starting Disposition 8. There are no other flat modifiers or Superiority. Backing supplies these points free, not extra Help dice (§10.2).

The guards” Grit 5 and 3 give their side 8 Disposition without a setup roll. Record both starting totals: they cap Defend and set the compromise grade (§10.2).

Commit the exchange. Before the first reveal, the PCs script Kade on Maneuver, Mira on Attack and Tarin on Defend. The guards script the first guard on Maneuver, the second on Defend and the first again on Feint. That’s legal: both guards act in the first round, and the second guard acts between the first guard’s turns (§10.5).

Volley 1 — Maneuver versus Maneuver. Both actions resolve independently. Kade uses Stealth +3 to slip around the first guard and open a path. His dice are 7 and 6, for a total of 16. The guard has Guard +1, so Kade’s target is Standard 15 + 1 = 16; no Situational Condition applies here. He meets it: 1 Power, plus 1 Tag Power from knows this district, for 2 Power. He buys Gain Position and lets the last point become Weal.

The guard’s independent Maneuver totals 15 against Standard 15: a success with 1 Power, which the GM spends on Impede.

Neither Maneuver changes Disposition. Gain Position and Impede both wait for the PCs” next scripted action; neither applies to a Resistance roll in between.

Volley 2 — Attack versus Defend. Mira receives Gain Position’s bonus die. She rolls 9, 6 and 2, keeps 9 + 6, adds Fight +3, then subtracts 3 for Impede: total 15. The guard’s Defend totals 13.

Mira wins by 2: 1 Power for winning plus 1 Tag Power from her weapon’s +1 Attack tag, 2 Power. She commits both to Attack, removing 3 shared Disposition (2 Power + 1), and the guards fall from 8 to 5. Their losing Defend restores nothing, and no individual guard is hurt or removed by the loss. Both pending Maneuver benefits are now spent (§10.6).

Volley 3 — Defend versus Feint. Tarin’s Defend is trumped: no roll, and no Disposition restored. The first guard’s Feint rolls independently and succeeds with 2 Power, committed to remove 3 PC Disposition.

Before applying that loss, Tarin accepts a new lasting sprained shoulder-3, describing how he holds the gate open while the others push through. The injury follows from the action and preserves the side’s position. A tier-3 injury cancels 2 incoming loss, so the PCs lose only 1 Disposition, falling from 8 to 7. Tarin marks his once-per-conflict injury trade used; at most one participant could trade against this Feint (§10.11).

The injury is a protected payment: armour, Resistance, redirection and incidental clearing can’t touch it, and later healing doesn’t refund the trade (§10.7). Below tier 6, it doesn’t take him out.

Continue or settle. After the exchange, the PCs have 7 Disposition and the guards 5, so neither side has won. Rotation carries on into the next exchange: Tarin acted last, so he can’t take its first volley. A brief pause doesn’t refresh Disposition or Tarin’s injury trade.

Suppose the PCs later win with 4 of their starting 8 Disposition left. They lost exactly half, so the compromise is Significant. That calls for one outcome-first package, not an automatic tier-3 injury, and Tarin’s existing injury can be credited once against it. The PCs achieve their stated intent, not permission to seize unrelated property or kill the guards (§10.12–10.13).

Afterward. Fresh Disposition in a new conflict won’t refill pools or heal Tarin’s shoulder. Treatment removes at most one tier of a lasting injury before a healing interval removes another (§6.6). A Taken Out character who is healed still needs explicit permission to rejoin an ongoing conflict (§6.4).

14.1 Known Risks

  • Currency load: pools + Tychē + Weal + Woe is the upper limit for medium crunch. Keep spend menus short on the sheet.

  • Modifier range: max is skill +4, helpful status up to +6, helpful Situational Condition up to +6, Tychē invoke +2. The two slots together range from −12 to +12 and are the largest swing. Playtest a ±3 cap on either slot, and whether 2 Power per rating prices broad Situational Conditions fairly.

  • Effort for Power may make high-Edge characters very efficient. Watch Edge-3 pools where Effort is free; consider a minimum cost of 1.

  • Resistance dice: Edge adds Resistance dice instead of reducing the cost, and only a double 12 is free (§6). Watch how often stacked penalties (two −1 Edge penalties on a 0-Edge pool) push Resistance to negative Edge, whether high-Edge pools drain faster than before, and whether the double-12 result (1–6%) is too rare to matter.

  • Four pools means more pool points to track. The sheet should put all four in one row with Edge beside each.

  • Conflict length: with scripting used for every big contest, the conflict test (§10.1) is what stops overuse.

  • Running dry: running dry imposes a status at tier 1 + overrun, and Scars come from tier 6, not from an empty pool (§6.1–6.3). Playtest the 2-points-per-tier depletion rate, the one-Scar-per-scene limit, and how often Resisting on an empty pool reaches tier 4 or higher.

  • Damage cycle: watch whether the injury trade (§10.11) is a hard choice or always taken, and whether it drags conflicts out. Test the healing intervals, the 3-segment Dying clock, and whether the 1-tier treatment limit makes Restore-cost abilities (Field Medic, Field Surgeon) too weak against lasting injuries.

  • Shared Disposition: one track per side (§10.2, §10.9). Watch whether a lone remaining participant acting repeatedly feels heroic or too forgiving, and whether larger parties get too many injury trades.

  • Old simulation figures: earlier notes reported mirrored fights about 23.5% shorter with shared Disposition, and an older setup averaging about 6 Disposition (range 3–9) and 6 volleys. Those figures predate the divide-by-three setup; re-run them before predicting conflict length. A fixed track of 5 is a possible comparison, not a rule.

  • Attack and Feint +1 (§10.6): watch whether Defend keeps pace, whether Feint becomes the default action, and how the +1 stacks with Crowd Work and Every Trick in the Book.

  • Superiority (§10.3): watch whether preparation becomes routine, how often both sides claim it, and whether one advantage is being described several ways to claim more categories.

  • Specialisations and Knowledge (§3.3): watch how often the Specialisation die applies, whether it changes the kept pair, whether it saves Effort or Woe, and how often the +2 cap makes it redundant. Narrow an overbroad Specialisation before changing the bonus. If players keep asking whether knowledge gives +1, clarify the wording rather than adding a modifier. Check that contacts create choices, that signature items matter without more routine bonus dice, and that consequences leave successful characters something to act on.

  • Specialists in scripted conflicts: if a character has no legal specialist contribution under the action lists, treat it as an action-design question; don’t let a Specialisation override the script.

  • Prepared in Advance (§4.2): watch Tychē use and overlap with the Furcifer’s ★ Flashback.

  • Playtest flags in the text: §2.1 status and Condition range; §5.1 stacking; §5.2 Condition accumulation; §6 negative Edge; §9.2 dangerous-circumstance Woe cap; §10.8 Floor Collapse and Make a Hazard prices; §6.3 one Scar per scene; §10.3 PC/opposition symmetry and Superiority eligibility; §10.5 Feint versus Maneuver; §10.13 four-step compromise; §10.17 area effects. These notes don’t settle them.
